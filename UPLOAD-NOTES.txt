across all 29 batches, then applied the precise test that actually worked:

    does the page NAME products, with prices, that it has no way to sell?

Not "does it have few affiliate links" — that flags every policy page and
category hub. The defect is the internal contradiction.

Twelve candidates. **Eleven discarded.**

naming the Bushnell Launch Pro, /titleist-pro-v1-review/ naming Chrome Tour and

── THE ONE REAL FINDING, AND IT IS SMALL ─────────────────────────────────────
/how-to-get-your-kid-into-golf/ — 84 inbound links, 3 affiliate links.

In "Gear on a Budget":

    "If they stick with it past 6 months, upgrade to a Callaway XJ ($350) or
     TaylorMade Team TM ($400)."

That is an explicit upgrade recommendation, by name, with prices. Neither is
buyable on the page, and the page's only buy path is the US Kids UL7 starter
set — which is the set for the *earlier* stage. A parent whose kid has stuck
with it is told exactly what to buy next and given nothing to click.

**The fix is one internal link, not an affiliate button.** /best-junior-golf-
clubs/ already exists and its sections are literally titled "Best Overall:
Callaway XJ Junior Set", "Best Value: TaylorMade Team TM" and "When to
Upgrade". It sells both products and covers the exact moment this sentence
describes. The tutorial did not link to it at all.

    now: "...or TaylorMade Team TM ($400) — both are covered in our
          [junior golf club guide]."

Anchor text "junior golf club guide" — descriptive, and it adds an internal
link from an 84-inbound page into a buying guide, which helps the link graph
as well as the reader.

I deliberately did NOT add affiliate buttons here. Two reasons: a section
carries one affiliateKey and I needed two products, and hand-writing affiliate
anchors into prose would bypass the click tracking that validate-click-tracking
checks. An internal link to the page that already sells them is both safer and
editorially better on an advice page.
